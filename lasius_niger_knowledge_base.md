# *Lasius niger* — Base Knowledge Reference

Version 1.0 · Compiled 28 Aug 2026
Scope: biology, ecology, behaviour, and captive husbandry of the black garden ant, assembled as a foundation for building a keeping/observation framework.

Confidence markers used throughout:
**[E]** = well established in literature · **[C]** = consensus among keepers, weakly sourced · **[?]** = contested or variable between sources

---

## 1. Identity and taxonomy

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Binomial | *Lasius niger* (Linnaeus, 1758) |
| Family / subfamily | Formicidae / Formicinae |
| Genus / subgenus | *Lasius* / subgenus *Lasius* (type species) |
| Common names | Black garden ant, common black ant |
| Key revisions | Seifert 1991 (split from *L. platythorax*); Wilson 1955 (monograph); Seifert 2018, 2020 (modern revision) |

**Critical ID caveat.** What older literature and most hobby sources call "*Lasius niger*" is a species complex. The 1991 split separated *L. niger* (open, sunny, disturbed habitats — gardens, pavements, meadows) from *L. platythorax* (woodland, damp, deadwood). **[E]** Related confusables in the *niger* group: *L. emarginatus*, *L. alienus*, *L. psammophilus*, *L. brunneus*. North American "*L. niger*" records are largely *L. neoniger* / *L. americanus*. **[E]**

Practical implication: a queen caught on a forest edge may not be *L. niger*. Reliable separation needs microscopy (pubescence density, scape/tibia setae counts). For a keeping framework, treat species ID as a field with a confidence level, not a certainty.

## 2. Morphology

| Caste | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Worker | 3–5 mm | Monomorphic; dark brown to black, matte, fine silvery pubescence; single petiole node; no spines; no sting (sprays formic acid from acidopore) |
| Queen | 8–11 mm (some sources 7–9) **[?]** | Stout gaster, wing scars after dealation, same colouration |
| Male | 3.5–5 mm | Slender, small head, large eyes, wasp-like; dies shortly after mating |

Nanitics (first-generation workers) are noticeably smaller — roughly ⅓ to ½ the size of later workers — because the founding queen has finite reserves. Worker size increases across successive cohorts as colony nutrition improves. **[E]**

Callows (freshly eclosed workers) are pale white and darken to black over roughly an hour. Useful as a visual marker for dating new eclosions.

## 3. Distribution and habitat

- Native and dominant across the Palaearctic: most of Europe, into Asia. Introduced/present in parts of North America, South America, Australasia. **[E]**
- One of the most abundant ants in Europe; a strong "cultural follower" — thrives in gardens, lawns, pavement cracks, urban waste ground.
- Nests in soil, under stones and slabs, in lawns (producing the classic crater mounds), occasionally in wall cavities and under paving.
- Nest depth reported to ~2 m in mature colonies. **[C]**
- Tolerates a wide thermal and moisture range; this generalism is exactly why it is the standard beginner species.

## 4. Colony structure and demography

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Queen number | Monogynous — one functional queen in mature colonies **[E]** |
| Queen mating | Polyandrous (multiply mated) **[E]** |
| Founding | Fully claustral **[E]** |
| Pleometrosis (co-founding) | ~18% of foundations, usually pairs; queens actively seek each other rather than associating at random **[E]** |
| Mean mature colony size | ~5,500 workers (Stradling 1970; Brian 1977; Beckers et al. 1989) **[E]** |
| Typical range cited | 4,000–7,000; up to 10,000–15,000; exceptional records 40,000–50,000 **[?]** |
| Worker lifespan | Nanitics ~3–6 months; later workers up to ~12–14 months **[C]** |
| Queen lifespan | Commonly 10–15 years; record ~28–29 years in captivity — longest recorded for any eusocial insect **[E]** |
| Worker reproduction | Worker-produced males occur even in queenright colonies **[E]** |
| Foraging strategy | Mass recruiter **[E]** |

Note on the size numbers: sources disagree substantially. The ~5,500 mean is the best-sourced figure; hobby claims of 50,000 are outliers and should be treated as ceilings, not expectations.

## 5. Annual cycle and phenology (temperate Europe)

| Period | Colony state |
|---|---|
| Mar–Apr | Emergence from diapause; queen resumes laying; overwintered larvae resume development |
| May–Aug | Peak brood rearing; highest protein demand; heavy aphid tending |
| Late Jun – early Sep (peak Jul–Aug) | Nuptial flights ("flying ant day") **[E]** |
| Sep–Oct | Brood wind-down; fat accumulation; foraging declines |
| Nov–Feb/Mar | Diapause; queen ceases oviposition |

**Nuptial flight triggers.** Hot, humid, anticyclonic afternoons and early evenings with low wind, often after a brief shower. Flights are highly synchronised regionally. Mating occurs in flight; the pair falls, separates, and the queen dealates and seeks cover immediately. **[E]**

**Wild vs captive founding timing.** In the field, first eggs are often not laid until the following spring (Eidmann 1926) — the autumn-caught queen overwinters first. In captivity, kept warm, queens typically lay within days to weeks. Both are normal; do not treat a non-laying autumn queen as failed. **[E]**

## 6. Brood development

Four stages: egg → larva (3 moults) → pupa (spins a cocoon — *L. niger* has cocooned, not naked, pupae) → adult. Eggs are tiny, white, kidney-shaped, with a sticky surface so they can be carried in clumps. **[E]**

### Reported development times (variable by source and temperature)

| Stage | Range A | Range B | Range C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg → larva | 9–16 d | 10–20 d | 10–12 d |
| Larva → pupa | 9–13 d | 14–24 d | 10–15 d |
| Pupa → adult | 9–12 d | 14–21 d | 10–25 d |
| **Total egg → worker** | **4–6 wk (up to 10)** | **6–10 wk** | **8–10 wk** |

**[?]** The spread is real: development is strongly temperature-dependent and the sources are not measuring at matched temperatures. For any framework, log temperature alongside stage transitions rather than trusting a fixed number.

### Temperature and development
- Development rate rises roughly linearly with temperature between the lower threshold and ~27 °C. Kipyatkov & Lopatina (2004) fitted lower temperature thresholds (LTT) and sums of effective temperatures (SET) for founding-queen brood. **[E]**
- **Populations differ by latitude.** Northern (St. Petersburg) populations develop more slowly than southern (Belgorod) ones at 17–20 °C but faster at 22–27 °C, and have a higher LTT and lower SET. Low temperatures also induce larval diapause more readily in northern stock. **[E]**
  → Practical consequence: development-rate tables are not transferable between source populations. Locally caught queens may behave differently from imported ones.
- Founding queens actively exploit warm microsites to accelerate the first brood; in the field they move brood to the surface under sun-warmed slabs in summer. **[E]**
- Acute heat stress on newly mated queens shortly after the nuptial flight measurably impairs founding success. **[E]**

## 7. Founding biology

- **Fully claustral**: the queen seals herself in, does not forage, and metabolises fat body and histolysed wing muscle to produce the first brood. **[E]**
- Queens may eat some of their own eggs as a resource-recycling measure — this is normal, not necessarily a failure signal. **[E]**
- Despite being claustral, queens *will* take food if offered, and supplementary carbohydrate and/or protein during founding significantly increases pupae and nanitic worker output (Biol. J. Linn. Soc. 2024). **[E]**
  → This contradicts the standard hobby rule "never feed a claustral queen". The safer synthesis: feeding is not *required* and adds mould and disturbance risk, but a tiny sugar droplet is measurably beneficial and not harmful in itself. Treat as an optional, low-dose intervention with a clear cost/benefit tradeoff.
- **Pleometrosis** is an active choice, not a density artefact — given two empty chambers, queens cluster far more than random assortment predicts. **[E]**
- Co-founding queens show **prophylactic undertaking behaviour**: if a co-foundress dies, the survivor bites, buries and removes the corpse, and does so *before* it becomes infectious, which measurably lowers her own risk of contracting the fungal pathogen. **[E]**
- Co-founding does **not** confer social immunity — paired queens do not allogroom and do not survive fungal challenge better than solitary ones. **[E]**
- Once nanitics eclose, the colony reduces to one queen; supernumerary queens are killed, typically leaving the most fecund. **[E]**
- Incipient colonies also raid neighbouring incipient nests for brood, sequestering foreign larvae and pupae to boost early growth. **[E]**

## 8. Foraging, communication, cognition

This is one of the best-studied ants in the world for collective behaviour — a rich seam if the framework includes any experimental or observational component.

- **Mass recruitment** via trail pheromone, laid from the hindgut.
- Pheromone deposition is **non-uniform**: ants deposit up to **22× more pheromone within 10 cm of a food source** than near the nest, and up to ~4× more on longer trails than short ones. **[E]**
- Ants **do not** upregulate pheromone to overwrite an erroneous trail — trails cannot be actively erased, only left to evaporate. **[E]**
- Deposition **decreases** when ants meet nestmates on the trail or at the food, a crowding-feedback mechanism. **[E]**
- **Route memory**: experienced foragers achieve ~95% correct choices at a single bifurcation and will prioritise private memory over pheromone when confident. Errors increase on routes requiring alternating turns vs repeating turns. **[E]**
- **Home-range marking** (passively deposited cuticular hydrocarbons) amplifies pheromone recruitment independently of trail pheromone. **[E]**
- **Task-related response differences**: scouts respond least to area marking and trail pheromone, recruits most, patrollers intermediate. **[E]** (Note: a separate study found trail-*following* fidelity itself is robust and unmodulated by distance, direction, or recent food experience — the modulation is in deposition and motivation, not in following accuracy. **[E]**)
- Bidirectional trail traffic is temporally organised to avoid jamming; polydomous colonies use trunk trails to exchange food and brood between nests. **[E]**
- Standard lab diet in the literature: **Bhatkar–Whitcomb diet** plus 1 M sucrose, ad libitum water. Lab colonies are often queenless fragments of 500–1000 workers in Fluon-coated boxes over plaster of Paris.

## 9. Nutrition

**Wild diet**: honeydew (dominant carbohydrate), floral and extrafloral nectar, scavenged and predated arthropods, seeds occasionally.

**Carbohydrates** — driving adult worker energy and forager activity.
**Protein** — driving larval growth and queen fecundity. Demand peaks May–August. **[E]**

Captive options:
- Sugar: honey water, sugar water (commonly 1:3 to 1:4), ant nectar products, jelly.
- Protein: freshly killed mealworms, crickets, fruit flies, roach nymphs; cricket mash; some keepers use cooked chicken/ham (higher spoilage risk).
- Water: constant access, non-negotiable, including through diapause.

**Sugar–predation coupling (important and under-appreciated).** When *L. niger* is given an alternative sugar source, its **predation rate on tended aphids increased eightfold**, while alternative *prey* had no significant effect. **[E]** Carbohydrate availability, not protein availability, governs how the colony treats a trophobiont partner. Directly relevant to any bioactive or aphid-inclusive setup.

Feeding frequency guidance (hobby consensus, **[C]**): sugar available continuously; protein 1–3× weekly scaled to brood load; remove uneaten protein within ~24–48 h.

## 10. Aphid mutualism (trophobiosis)

- *L. niger* is **the** commonest ant in European aphid colonies. **[E]**
- Ants palpate aphids to elicit honeydew; in exchange they defend aphids from predators and parasitoids, and will relocate aphids to better host plants. They also overwinter aphid eggs in the nest.
- **Partner recognition is chemical**: ants discriminate myrmecophilous from non-myrmecophilous aphids using **cuticular hydrocarbons**, not just honeydew presence — non-partners are treated as prey. **[E]**
- **Honeydew quality drives attendance.** Ant attendance tracks both honeydew volume and sugar composition, particularly the trisaccharides **melezitose and raffinose** (20–35% of sugars in preferred species). Measured production rates: *Metopeurum fuscoviride* 880 µg/aphid/h ≫ *Brachycaudus cardui* 223 ≫ *Aphis fabae* 133 ≫ *Macrosiphoniella tanacetaria* 46 — and attendance ranked in the same order. **[E]**
- The relationship sits on a mutualism–exploitation continuum: ants both farm and eat the same aphids, with the balance set by their own sugar supply (see §9).

## 11. Interspecific relations

**As host to temporary social parasites.** *Lasius* subgenus *Chthonolasius* (e.g. *L. umbratus*, *L. mixtus*, *L. sabularum*) found colonies by invading a *niger*-group nest, eliminating the resident queen, and having host workers raise their brood. **[E]**
- Mechanism recently described: parasitic queens covertly approach the host queen and spray abdominal fluid (apparently formic acid) at her, which triggers the **host workers to kill their own mother** — chemically induced matricide. Documented in *L. orientalis*/*L. japonicus* and *L. umbratus*/*L. flavus*; *L. niger* workers killing their queen after *L. umbratus* introduction was reported as early as Crawley 1909. **[E]**
- *L. fuliginosus* is a **hyperparasite** — parasitic on *Chthonolasius*, which are themselves parasitic. Its association with *L. niger* is reported but Seifert (2018) holds it does not survive in *niger* nests. **[?]**

**Pathogens and parasites.**
- Entomopathogenic fungi: *Metarhizium brunneum*, *M. pingshaense* — the standard experimental pathogens; naturally soil-borne.
- *Ophiocordyceps*-type fungi (behaviour-manipulating) — remove infected workers immediately.
- Cestode: *Mesocestoides* sp., indirect transmission outside the nest.
- Phorid flies, nematodes, and mites occur in wild colonies.

**Competitors.** Other *Lasius*, *Myrmica*, *Formica*, *Tetramorium* in shared habitat. Newly mated queens avoid areas patrolled by established-colony workers, which concentrates new foundations into gaps and drives the high local competition that favours pleometrosis and brood raiding. **[E]**

## 12. Captive husbandry parameters

### Consolidated targets

| Parameter | Founding | Active colony | Diapause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nest temperature | 20–22 °C **[C]** | 22–25 °C (up to 26) **[C]** | 5–10 °C (tolerable 5–15) **[?]** |
| Outworld temperature | — | 18–30 °C | — |
| Nest humidity | High, tube-driven | ~50–70% **[?]** | keep from drying |
| Outworld humidity | — | 30–60% | — |
| Light | Dark | Ambient; nest shaded | Dark |
| Disturbance | Minimal, check every 5–7 days | Weekly | Minimal |

**[?]** on humidity: published hobby figures range from "10–50%" to "50–70%", which is not a real disagreement so much as different instruments and different definitions (relative humidity vs substrate moisture). Do not chase a number. Use **brood placement as the readout** — the colony moves brood to the moisture level it wants. This is the single most reliable humidity instrument available.

**[?]** on diapause temperature: sources give 5–8, 5–10, ~10, and 5–15 °C, with one explicitly warning against going much below 10 °C for long-term colony health. Wide tolerance; ~8–10 °C is the safe midpoint. Above 15 °C the colony does not properly enter diapause.

### Housing progression
1. **Test tube setup** — 5×150 mm glass tube, water reservoir behind a tight cotton plug, second cotton plug at the entrance. Sufficient for months. Founding and early colony.
2. **Tube + outworld** — once nanitics are present, place the tube in a Fluon/PTFE-lined arena and feed *only* in the arena. Never feed inside the tube.
3. **Small formicarium** — Ytong, plaster/gypsum, acrylic with a moisture layer, 3D-printed, or soil/sand-clay sandwich. Move only when the colony fills ~10–15% of the target space.
4. **Modular expansion** — add chambers/modules as the colony grows, keeping unopened chambers plugged.

### Escape control
Workers are fast, small, and persistent — this species is a genuine escape risk. PTFE/Fluon barrier around the top of the outworld, re-applied as it degrades; check all tubing joints and lid gaps.

**Species-specific quirk:** colonies commonly pack debris into the gap between the nest frame and the lid; over time this can build enough pressure to lift or snap the lid. Inspect and clear those gaps. **[C]**

## 13. Diapause protocol

1. **Trigger window:** roughly late October, ending late March. Minimum useful duration ~2 months; 3–4 months is typical.
2. **Ramp down:** reduce gradually — 0.2–0.3 °C/day at the careful end, or stepwise (living room → coolest room for a week → garage/cellar/wine fridge). Avoid a single abrupt drop.
3. **Hold:** ~8–10 °C, dark, insulated against frost. Never below ~3 °C.
4. **During:** no feeding required. **Water access is mandatory.** Optional token carbohydrate every month or two in case of early waking. Check tube water level; do not let it dry.
5. **Ramp up:** +1–2 °C/day back to room temperature over 7–10 days.

**Why it matters:** diapause is the only period the queen ceases oviposition and rests. Skipping it is repeatedly linked to shortened queen lifespan and reduced long-term egg yield. A temperate ant kept warm year-round burns out. **[C]**

**Exception commonly cited:** the *first* diapause may be skipped for a queen caught late in the season, to give the incipient colony a head start; diapause the following year. **[C]** Treat as a defensible tradeoff, not a free lunch.

## 14. Failure modes and troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| White/grey/green fuzzy growth on cotton or glass | Mould — food contamination, non-sterile cotton, over-wet | Prepare fresh tube; migrate queen by placing tubes adjacent in a dark box and letting her move herself |
| Black staining ("ant sludge") in tube | Normal waste breakdown | No action unless it spreads over the cotton barrier |
| Pink water or pink cotton | Bacterial growth | Act — transfer to clean tube |
| Tiny moving dots on ants, brood, cotton | Mites | Isolate the colony, stop sharing tools, remove old protein, reduce excess moisture, transfer to clean tube |
| Worker climbing high and locking mandibles, odd behaviour | Possible *Ophiocordyceps* | Remove infected individuals immediately, clean outworld thoroughly |
| Queen stops laying / eats brood | Disturbance, light, vibration, wrong temperature | Reduce checks to once weekly; darken; stabilise temperature |
| Colony not growing | Over-large nest too early, cold, insufficient protein, over-checking | Downsize housing, warm, feed protein, leave alone |
| Brood dying | Humidity or temperature out of range | Correct; use brood placement as the guide |
| Dead workers accumulating | Normal midden behaviour + finite worker lifespan | Clear the midden; only alarm if rate is high |

**Prevention hierarchy** (highest leverage first): sterile tube and fresh cotton every time → distilled water → no food inside the nest → remove uneaten protein within 24–48 h → quarantine anything new (colonies, natural materials) → do not share tools between colonies → check briefly, rarely, in low light.

**The dominant beginner failure is over-intervention**, not under-provision. Founding queens fail from disturbance far more often than from hunger.

## 15. Metrics worth capturing (framework input)

Grouped by what they are good for.

**Identity / provenance**
species ID + confidence, capture or purchase date, capture location, source population latitude (matters for development rates — see §6), pleometrosis (n queens at founding), date of dealation if observed.

**State (point-in-time)**
worker count (or estimate band), brood counts by stage (eggs / larvae / pupae), queen present & condition, callows present, nest occupancy fraction, midden location.

**Environment (continuous or spot)**
nest temperature, outworld temperature, humidity proxy, brood pile position (the real humidity readout), light exposure, vibration events.

**Events (timestamped)**
first egg, first larva, first pupa, first nanitic, each nest move, each diapause entry/exit, each feeding, each intervention, mortality events, escapes.

**Derived**
egg→worker interval at recorded temperature, growth rate (workers/week), protein acceptance rate, sugar consumption rate, brood:worker ratio, days since last disturbance.

**Why this list:** every entry above corresponds to something in §1–14 that is either (a) known to vary in ways published numbers don't capture, or (b) a leading indicator of a failure mode. That is the argument for tracking it rather than tracking everything.

## 16. Known unknowns and contested points

1. **Colony size ceiling** — ~5,500 mean is well sourced; 40,000–50,000 claims are not well supported.
2. **Queen lifespan distribution** — the ~29-year record is real but is a record, not an expectation. Median captive lifespan is poorly documented.
3. **Optimal diapause temperature** — sources span 5–15 °C with no strong comparative study cited.
4. **Feeding claustral queens** — experimentally beneficial, contradicting standard hobby doctrine. Risk/benefit not quantified for typical home setups.
5. **Humidity targets** — hobby figures are unstandardised and probably not comparable.
6. **Development-time tables** — none of the widely circulated ones state their temperature, and population-of-origin effects are substantial.
7. **Species identity in the hobby** — an unknown fraction of "*L. niger*" in captivity is *L. platythorax* or another *niger*-group species.

Any framework should treat these seven as parameters to be measured locally rather than constants to be looked up.
